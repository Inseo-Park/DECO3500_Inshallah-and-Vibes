# Secondary Research

## Literature Analysis

ECHO explores whether encountering campus stories and sharing personal memories can help UQ students connect with campus and one another. These five sources examine different parts of that opportunity: educational belonging, heritage interpretation, campus identity and storytelling across physical places. The analysis considers each source's evidence and limitations, then identifies implications for ECHO. These implications are design proposals; the papers do not evaluate ECHO itself.

### 1. Student belonging and relationships

Allen et al. (2021) review research on belonging, particularly student–teacher relationships, and extend the discussion to higher education. They associate belonging with acceptance, respect and support, and with educational engagement and wellbeing. Their analysis emphasises the quality of relationships: opportunities to meet people are insufficient if those interactions do not feel supportive.

The chapter provides a useful relational foundation, but much of its evidence concerns school students. Its synthesis also identifies limited tested interventions. Applying it to university students using an asynchronous installation therefore requires further evaluation; recognising another person's story is different from developing a supportive relationship (Allen et al., 2021).

**Implication for ECHO:** Stories, memories and replies should help students feel heard and recognised. Evaluation should ask whether users feel included and connected to contributors, alongside recording interactions. Contribution counts alone cannot establish belonging.

### 2. Cultural heritage, place attachment and cultural identity

Chan et al. (2024) survey local visitors to four revitalised heritage buildings in Hong Kong, reporting 348 valid questionnaires. Their structural equation model links perceived revitalisation quality with cultural identity, and cultural identity with place attachment. Knowledge transfer strengthens the first relationship, while cultural activity engagement does not significantly moderate the second.

This offers a possible mechanism connecting heritage interpretation and attachment. However, retrospective self-reports cannot establish causality, and local heritage visitors differ from UQ's student population. Reporting also warrants caution: the analysis section states 352 valid samples, conflicting with the abstract and sampling section (Chan et al., 2024).

**Implication for ECHO:** Explain why a place matters through specific people, events and memories. Test comprehension and connection separately: remembering a historical detail does not necessarily mean feeling attached to campus. This paper supports investigating that relationship, rather than assuming a campus-history intervention will produce it.

### 3. Universities, belonging and a sense of home

Fulford and Mahon (2025) develop a philosophical account of the university as a sanctuary that combines safety and familiarity with encounters that challenge established assumptions. Their argument draws on educational philosophy, sanctuary policies and a literary reading of Nan Shepherd's *The Quarry Wood*. Belonging can coexist with unfamiliarity and intellectual discomfort.

This complicates an approach that equates connection with making campus feel comfortable. Its strength is conceptual: it asks what kinds of encounters university life should enable. It is not an empirical study demonstrating that a lack of historical content causes loneliness, or that heritage storytelling reduces it (Fulford & Mahon, 2025).

**Implication for ECHO:** Include stories that invite curiosity about experiences beyond a user's own, including difficult or contested histories. Participation should remain voluntary, with enough context for reflection. A useful evaluation question is whether a story changes how users understand a familiar place, rather than only whether they enjoyed it.

### 4. Campus history and a sense of place

Peace and Shearer (2017) reflect on the Puke Ahu campus identity initiative at Massey University's Wellington campus. Archives, creative work, teaching resources and walking tours reveal histories obscured by everyday campus use. Their account treats place as culturally and politically layered, with multiple possible identifications. The Pākehā authors also examine their authority and responsibilities when engaging with Māori histories.

This is the closest campus precedent, but it is a reflective project account, without a controlled measure of increased student belonging. Workload barriers and initially limited student representation show that producing resources does not ensure participation (Peace & Shearer, 2017).

**Implication for ECHO:** Offer brief entry points within everyday campus routines, while allowing deeper exploration. Attribute sources and distinguish personal memories from archival claims. For UQ, Indigenous content would require collaboration with relevant custodians and knowledge holders; the Māori context cannot be directly transferred. These are proposed content practices, not evidence of consultation already undertaken.

### 5. Historical trails and physical exploration

Fagence (2017) uses the Ned Kelly story to develop a heritage "trailscape": a network of locations connected by a narrative, rather than necessarily one linear route. The case examines how dispersed sites and artefacts can convey a story despite differences between geographical order and historical chronology.

Its contribution is an interpretive approach to organising heritage experiences. It does not test whether walking a trail improves student belonging. Fagence (2017) also cautions that choices suited to this particular story may need substantial adaptation elsewhere. For ECHO, a further concern is that foregrounding a single compelling narrative could obscure other accounts of a location.

**Implication for ECHO:** Connect installations through related stories, with each location understandable on its own. Students could discover connections in whichever order fits their movements. Any exploration feature should be tested for accessibility and effort, rather than assuming users can or want to complete a prescribed route.

## Synthesis and Design Implications

The sources address distinct outcomes. Relational belonging concerns feeling accepted by others (Allen et al., 2021), while place attachment concerns connection to a setting (Chan et al., 2024). ECHO should investigate both without treating them as interchangeable. A student might learn about the Great Court yet feel no closer to other students; another might recognise a shared experience in someone's memory without learning campus history.

There is also a useful tension between narrative coherence and plurality. Fagence (2017) organises dispersed places around a story; Peace and Shearer (2017) foreground multiple identifications. Fulford and Mahon (2025) further complicate comfort as the aim. Our design response is to make individual stories easy to follow while keeping room for different, sometimes conflicting experiences of the same place.

Together, the analysis suggests three priorities for ECHO:

- **Make place specific:** Connect a story to something users can notice around them, and explain the connection clearly.
- **Make participation meaningful:** Let students encounter others' experiences, contribute their own memories and receive recognition, without requiring public disclosure or contribution.
- **Evaluate outcomes separately:** Assess story comprehension, connection to place, perceived inclusion and willingness to return. Follow-up research would help establish whether any effects persist beyond an initial interaction.

The resulting hypothesis is that understandable, situated stories and reciprocal participation may help students build connections. The next step is to test that hypothesis with ECHO's users; these sources provide a rationale, not confirmation of its effectiveness.
