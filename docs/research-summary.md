# Research and evaluation synthesis

This page brings together ECHO's research and evaluation findings. Findings describe the tested versions and do not establish that every issue was resolved in echo-tradeshow.

## Opportunity and intended users

The [interview plan](proposal/interview-questions.md) frames the opportunity as helping UQ students encounter the cultural, political, architectural and environmental histories of campus and develop a connection to place. It particularly identifies students early in their university experience, students interested in campus history, and those who lack a sense of belonging. These are intended audiences, rather than a verified description of every test participant.

Initial interviews suggest that connection already grows through familiar study spaces, architecture, walking, shared routines and time with other people. Some students notice campus features without knowing their stories, and competing study and work commitments leave little time for deliberate exploration. This supports bringing brief stories into places and routines that students already use. It does not show that learning history necessarily improves belonging.

## Findings and design implications

| Finding in the supplied evidence | Design implication | Confidence and open question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The early story-feed responses valued understandable navigation and easy comments/uploads. One reported silent audio; another asked for clearer category labels. | Preserve clear navigation and easy contribution while checking that advertised content works. | Two written responses about a largely simulated concept; they do not establish actual location detection or sustained engagement. |
| On 16 September, 8/10 sessions entered through Listen and 4/10 left an Echo; two contributions were written and two recorded. Some listeners later contributed. | Make discovery an easy entry point and allow contribution after browsing. Keep sharing optional. | Descriptive evidence from one prototype. It does not prove that listening caused contribution or predict uptake. |
| Of eight listeners, five selected video first and three audio. Only three sessions recorded a final media preference: two video, one both. Interviews also include a clear preference for reading and a need for subtitles. | Offer short readable content and accessible media choices; include captions and transcripts. | There is no universal preferred format. Three preference answers cannot establish a population preference; audio should not be removed on this evidence. |
| Interviewees valued encountering something left by another person. Contributions included recent experiences and campus wildlife, alongside interest in historical events. | Present ECHO as both historical discovery and a growing collection of lived campus memories. | This supports the appeal of an asynchronous exchange. Long-term connection, repeat use and community growth remain untested. |
| People described feeling watched or embarrassed by some large installation forms and suggested private phone access or a small, sheltered form. | Keep the physical interaction approachable, brief and voluntary. Explore personal-device access without assuming it replaces the shared installation. | Responses concerned concept images and facilitated prototypes; comfort needs field testing in the intended setting. |
| The Wizard-of-Oz records show uncertainty about what to contribute and interest in quick photo/voice participation and easier browsing. | Use concrete prompts and reduce effort between discovery and contribution. | A reel interface was discussed with a participant, not independently validated as the best solution. |
| October feedback reported confusing garden navigation, small Back controls, jitter, excessive zoom, blank-space selection, unavailable media and clipped labels. | Prioritise predictable navigation, selection and media behaviour before adding more social features. | Reported faults require reproduction on the current version and intended hardware. Their technical causes are not established by feedback. |
| P2 explicitly preferred the voxel garden for easier interaction and less tree overlap; P1 also found navigation less confusing. P3 liked the concept but raised styling and stability issues. | Use the voxel direction as a justified iteration candidate and retest after usability fixes. | Only one participant stated an explicit overall preference. This is not a four-person vote for a visual style. |
| October feedback valued pseudonymous participation, visible contributions and language options, and proposed maps, Q&A, notifications, polls and messaging. | Preserve understandable identity and discovery; investigate new social features separately. | Positive feedback and feature requests do not establish safety, accuracy, sustained use or implementation priority. |

## How the direction evolved

The supplied iteration deck records a move from coordinated games and a story feed to ECHO: a student can encounter a story alone and leave a trace for a later visitor. Feedback about time, comfort and media choice led to a smaller book/tablet installation, optional formats and shorter browsing. The October comparison then made garden legibility and interaction reliability central concerns. See the [design process](process/iterations.md) for the fuller chronology and [requirements](requirements.md) for traceable experience goals.

This gives a practical social/mobile interpretation: the campus place supplies context; contributions persist across time; the garden makes other people's participation visible; and shared or personal screens change privacy and access. A formal design-framework selection and its academic references still need to be documented by the team.

## Evaluation gaps

The evidence does not provide a controlled evaluation of echo-tradeshow, a longitudinal belonging/return-use measure, a complete participant/recruitment record, a complete literature bibliography, or a documented cultural-content review. The two original story-feed feedback documents contain participant names alongside consent wording promising anonymised responses. Review those originals and other identifiable research material before further public publication. These gaps are recorded without assuming that consent was absent or that testing did not occur.

For the next evaluation, observe first-time users discovering a story, submitting a memory, finding their contribution and encountering another person's contribution. Record version, device, task outcomes, assistance and participant counts separately from session counts. Include media accessibility and garden navigation; use follow-up interviews to explain behaviours rather than interpreting elapsed time alone.
